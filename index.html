Bishkek · winter 2026

Every winter, Bishkek loses its mountains.

Smoke from coal stoves, old cars and the power plant gets trapped over the city and hides the Ala-Too range. Everyone here breathes it, and children suffer first. The causes are measured, and the fixes are known.

Causes

Where the smog comes from

Home heating and cooking29%

About 150,000 households heated with coal in 2023. Low chimneys release smoke at roof height every evening, right where people breathe.

Traffic27%

About 700,000 cars now crowd roads built for 300,000. Most are over 10 years old, and many have no working catalytic converter.

From outside the city21%

Villages across the Chüy valley also burn coal, and wind brings in dust from the region. About a fifth of the city's PM2.5 starts outside it.

Power plant (CHP)11%

Bishkek's coal-fired CHP is the largest single source of emissions, but its 200 m stack spreads the smoke far, so its share at street level is smaller.

Dust9%

Unpaved roads, construction sites and quarries. Dust matters most in summer.

Industry and waste burning3%

Brick kilns, factories and rubbish fires. Small in total, but toxic for the people who live next to them.

Why the smoke can't escape

On winter nights, cold air sinks into the Chüy valley at the foot of the mountains. A layer of warmer air settles above it like a lid. With little wind, smoke from chimneys and exhausts piles up under the lid. Pollution peaks around 7 pm, when people come home and light their stoves.

The plan

Eight moves to clean air

Each percentage is the drop in PM2.5 the same 2024 model gives for that measure. Together, these measures could cut Bishkek's PM2.5 by 50–70% in the medium to long term.

Biggest cuts

Most of the smog comes from three sources. These moves go after them.

Get homes off coal

Bring gas to the 58 residential areas that still have none, and fit heat pumps or electric heating where pipes can't reach. Over 90% of the country's electricity comes from hydropower, and ground-source heat pumps already working in Bishkek make 4.2 kWh of heat from each kWh of power.

Switching all ~150,000 coal homes at $5,000 each would cost about $750 million, close to 7% of GDP. Climate funds, cheap loans and buying equipment in bulk can bring the cost down for families.

up to 29%
Feasibility
High for gas, harder for heat pumps

Mayor's office · Ministry of Energy

Clean up car exhaust

Yearly emission checks, a ban on removing catalytic converters, limits on importing old cars and a low-emission zone in the centre. Keep replacing diesel minibuses with gas and electric buses.

PM2.5 cut
3–6% from cars, 13% from all transport measures
Feasibility
High to moderate
Who leads
Ministry of Transport and Communications · Mayor's office

Fix the power plant

Raising its filters' efficiency from 98% to 99% would halve the plant's particle emissions. Its stack emissions should be published online in real time. Switching it to gas would cut the city's PM2.5 by about 9%.

up to 9%
Feasibility

Who leads
Ministry of Energy

Quick wins

Smaller cuts, but cheap and fast to start.

Make the bus the fast choice

Bus lanes on the main streets, frequent routes to the outskirts and cheap passes for students. Every full bus takes dozens of cars off the road.

Insulate first

Insulate the walls, roofs and windows of coal-heated homes. Each home then needs less fuel, whatever it burns, and the family pays less.

It has been done

Cities that cleared their air

Bishkek is not the first city to choke on coal smoke. These cities show what works, and what to avoid.

Kraków, Poland

Ban on coal in homes · 2019

Banned coal and wood in home stoves from September 2019, after years of helping families replace old stoves. By the 2019/20 heating season, PM2.5 and PM10 were about 45% lower than in 2012/13.

Notes from Poland, 2021

Beijing, China

Clean air action plan · from 2013

Moved homes and boilers from coal to gas and electricity, and cleaned up traffic and industry. Yearly PM2.5 fell by 66% between 2013 and 2024. Coal's share of the city's PM2.5 dropped from 22% to 3% in four years.

Beijing Municipal Government, 2025; UNEP, 2019

Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia

Ban on raw coal · 2019

Banned raw coal in homes in May 2019 and switched families to briquettes. PM2.5 fell by about a fifth in the first winter, but carbon-monoxide poisonings rose sharply. The lesson: a fuel ban must come with safe stoves and CO alarms.

Smart Air, 2020; Bulletin of the WHO, 2023

What they share: a clear ban, money to help families switch, and steady enforcement.

What you can do

Protect your family today

Read the air

Apps such as IQAir show Bishkek's AQI. Move the slider to the number you see.

170 Unhealthy ≈ 92 µg/m³ of PM2.5
Everyone

Children, older people, people with asthma or heart disease

Check the air now: IQAir AQICN

  • Wear a real mask on smog days

    Choose an N95, KN95 or FFP2 respirator and fit it tightly. Cloth and surgical masks don't stop fine particles.

  • Keep windows shut in the evening

    Smoke peaks around 7 pm. Air your rooms when the readings are lowest, often in the afternoon.

  • Clean the air at home

    A HEPA air purifier in the bedroom matters most, especially in a child's room.

  • Burn only permitted fuel

    Never burn rubbish, plastic or tyres. Fine coal (0–13 mm) is banned. If you heat with coal or gas, fit a carbon-monoxide alarm.

  • Switch your heating with a cheap loan

    Gas connection: up to 150,000 som at 3% a year (ABank, Eldik Bank). Heat pumps, electric boilers or solar: up to 1,000,000 som from 6% a year (Eldik Bank, ABank, Bakai Bank).

    Source: Bishkek mayor's office, November 2026. Terms can change, so check with the bank.

  • Drive less, and never idle

    Take the bus when you can, switch the engine off while you wait, and keep your catalytic converter.
